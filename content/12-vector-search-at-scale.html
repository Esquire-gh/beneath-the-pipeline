12 · How vector search scales

This module breaks module 7's brute-force nearest neighbour. This is what Chroma was doing in Part 0, and what it decided on your behalf.

What this module is about

Finding the nearest vectors by brute force means comparing the query against every vector in the collection. The cost grows in a straight line with the corpus, and unlike the inverted index there is no exact shortcut: nothing about a query vector tells you which stored vectors you can safely ignore. A million vectors means a million comparisons, every query, forever.

The way out is the same move as module 7, arrangement in advance, applied to a harder problem. HNSW, short for hierarchical navigable small world, builds a graph over the vectors where each one is connected to some of its neighbours, in several layers. The top layer is sparse and lets you cross the whole space in a few hops. Each layer down is denser and more local. A search enters at the top, greedily walks towards the query, drops a layer, walks again, and finishes with a careful local search at the bottom.

Nothing about that walk is guaranteed to arrive at the true nearest neighbours. It can head into a region that looks good locally and miss a better one elsewhere. How hard it looks before giving up is a parameter called ef_search, and that single integer is a dial between speed and correctness. This module has you build the graph, sweep the dial, and measure both ends, including the case the sales pages do not mention: what happens when someone adds an ordinary filter to the query.

Where did the speed come from, and where exactly did the correctness go?

Build it yourself

There are three things to build. First the truth: brute force over every vector, which module 7's TODO 4 already gave you. That is what "right" means here, and you need it before you can say anything about approximate. Then an HNSW index over the same vectors. Then the measurement that matters, recall@10: the fraction of the genuinely nearest ten that the index actually returned.

two different questions Recall here is measured against brute force, not against MS MARCO's judgments. It asks whether the index found what it was asked to find. Module 8's NDCG asks whether that was worth finding. An index can have perfect recall and terrible NDCG: it faithfully returned the ten nearest vectors, and the ten nearest vectors were not the answer. Both numbers appear below, and keeping them apart is the point.

Why it behaves this way

The filter result is the one to understand, because it is the one that will bite you in production, and it has a mechanism, not a mystery.

The graph was built over all the vectors. Its edges encode which vectors are near which others across the whole collection, and the walk follows those edges. When you filter afterwards, you are asking the graph to find the ten nearest documents and then throwing most of them away, and the ten nearest documents overall are hardly ever in a bucket holding {{ 12-vector-search-at-scale.filter.selectivity | pct }} of the collection. So you must ask for far more than ten and hope enough survive, which means walking far more of the graph, which is exactly the cost the graph existed to avoid.

Meanwhile, once the filter is selective enough, the honest answer is very cheap. There are only {{ 12-vector-search-at-scale.filter.matching_documents | int }} candidates, so compare against all of them. Brute force over a small set beats a clever structure over a large one, and there is a crossover point where that stops being true. Vector databases spend real engineering on this: pre-filtering with per-attribute subgraphs, filtered graph traversal that only follows edges to matching nodes, or detecting the selective case and scanning instead. When a vector database's documentation talks about filter strategies or a selectivity threshold, this measurement is what it is about.

Two kinds of "faster", one module apart

Module 11's WAND skipped work and returned identical answers, because it could prove that what it skipped could not win. This module's graph skips work and returns different answers, because it cannot prove anything. It is a walk with a budget.

Both are called making retrieval fast. Only one of them needs a recall measurement next to it, and the one that does is the one everybody reaches for first.

If what you saw didn't match

If your recall is 1.0000 at every setting, your corpus is probably too small for the graph to have any choices to get wrong. Under a few tens of thousands of vectors, HNSW usually finds everything. Run at --scale part2 or larger before drawing conclusions from the curve.

If HNSW is slower than brute force, that is a real result at small scale and not a mistake. Brute force is one large matrix multiply, which modern hardware is extremely good at. The graph walk is many small dependent lookups, which it is not. The crossover is somewhere in the hundreds of thousands of vectors on most machines. Find yours.

If hnswlib will not install on macOS, it compiles C++ and needs the libc++ headers on the compiler's search path. requirements.txt has the one-line export that fixes it.

If your filtered recall looks fine at low ef_search, check that your filter is actually selective. A filter matching half the corpus costs the graph almost nothing. The cliff is a property of selectivity, not of filtering.
