Part III · module 12 · build · 50–80 minutes

12 · How vector search scales

This module breaks module 7's brute-force nearest neighbour. This is what Chroma was doing in Part 0, and what it decided on your behalf.

What this module is about

Finding the nearest vectors by brute force means comparing the query against every vector in the collection. The cost grows in a straight line with the corpus, and unlike the inverted index there is no exact shortcut: nothing about a query vector tells you which stored vectors you can safely ignore. A million vectors means a million comparisons, every query, forever.

The way out is the same move as module 7, arrangement in advance, applied to a harder problem. HNSW, short for hierarchical navigable small world, builds a graph over the vectors where each one is connected to some of its neighbours, in several layers. The top layer is sparse and lets you cross the whole space in a few hops. Each layer down is denser and more local. A search enters at the top, greedily walks towards the query, drops a layer, walks again, and finishes with a careful local search at the bottom.

Nothing about that walk is guaranteed to arrive at the true nearest neighbours. It can head into a region that looks good locally and miss a better one elsewhere. How hard it looks before giving up is a parameter called ef_search, and that single integer is a dial between speed and correctness. This module has you build the graph, sweep the dial, and measure both ends, including the case the sales pages do not mention: what happens when someone adds an ordinary filter to the query.

The puzzle

Brute force over 1,000,000 vectors takes 21.9ms per query, 384,000,000 multiply-adds every time, and it is exactly right.

HNSW answers the same query in 106µs and quietly stops guaranteeing the right answer. At its cheapest setting it returns 86% of the ten nearest vectors.

Where did the speed come from, and where exactly did the correctness go?

Build it yourself

There are three things to build. First the truth: brute force over every vector, which module 7's TODO 4 already gave you. That is what "right" means here, and you need it before you can say anything about approximate. Then an HNSW index over the same vectors. Then the measurement that matters, recall@10: the fraction of the genuinely nearest ten that the index actually returned.

two different questions Recall here is measured against brute force, not against MS MARCO's judgments. It asks whether the index found what it was asked to find. Module 8's NDCG asks whether that was worth finding. An index can have perfect recall and terrible NDCG: it faithfully returned the ten nearest vectors, and the ten nearest vectors were not the answer. Both numbers appear below, and keeping them apart is the point.

What the approximation costs where users can feel it

the same queries, scored with module 8's harness

10 0.8573 0.5989

Losing 86% of the nearest neighbours cost 0.0704 of NDCG. Those are not the same size of loss, and the reason is worth holding onto: a "wrong" neighbour returned by the graph is usually the eleventh or twelfth nearest, not a random document, and the eleventh nearest is often just as good an answer. Recall is the strict measure. NDCG is the one your users experience.

And then somebody asks for a filter

This is the most ordinary request there is: search only this customer's documents, only this year, only this language. Here every vector gets a bucket and the query asks for one bucket, 10,035 of 1,000,000 documents, or 1.0%.

strategyresults kept of 10 recall@10median
graph, ef = 10 0.10 0.0100 107µs
graph, ef = 256 2.81 0.2807 1.1ms
graph, ef = 4096 10.00 0.9970 13.6ms
ignore the graph, scan the matches 10.00 1.0000 194µs

At the setting that was fine a moment ago, the graph returns 0.10 usable results out of ten and recall of 0.0100. Pushing ef_search up to 4096 recovers 100% recall and costs 13.6ms per query.

And scanning the 10,035 matching vectors directly, with no graph and no approximation, takes 194µs and is exactly right.

Why it behaves this way

The filter result is the one to understand, because it is the one that will bite you in production, and it has a mechanism, not a mystery.

The graph was built over all the vectors. Its edges encode which vectors are near which others across the whole collection, and the walk follows those edges. When you filter afterwards, you are asking the graph to find the ten nearest documents and then throwing most of them away, and the ten nearest documents overall are hardly ever in a bucket holding 1.0% of the collection. So you must ask for far more than ten and hope enough survive, which means walking far more of the graph, which is exactly the cost the graph existed to avoid.

Meanwhile, once the filter is selective enough, the honest answer is very cheap. There are only 10,035 candidates, so compare against all of them. Brute force over a small set beats a clever structure over a large one, and there is a crossover point where that stops being true. Vector databases spend real engineering on this: pre-filtering with per-attribute subgraphs, filtered graph traversal that only follows edges to matching nodes, or detecting the selective case and scanning instead. When a vector database's documentation talks about filter strategies or a selectivity threshold, this measurement is what it is about.

What Part 0 was doing

Go back to db.add() and db.query(). Chroma built an index of this kind, this graph or one very like it, with an M, an ef_construction, and an ef_search chosen for you. Your Part 0 pipeline has a recall number. You have never seen it. It is a specific value on the curve above, and it was picked by someone who had never met your corpus.

That is not a criticism of the library. Defaults have to be something, and these are reasonable ones. It is a statement about what you now know how to ask: what is my recall, and what would it cost me to raise it?

Two kinds of "faster", one module apart

Module 11's WAND skipped work and returned identical answers, because it could prove that what it skipped could not win. This module's graph skips work and returns different answers, because it cannot prove anything. It is a walk with a budget.

Both are called making retrieval fast. Only one of them needs a recall measurement next to it, and the one that does is the one everybody reaches for first.

How this fits the pipeline

Upward: the retrieve() line has a quality setting inside it that nothing in the API surfaces. Backward: module 6 said distance is a promise the model makes, and this module is where the index stops keeping that promise exactly. Module 7's brute force is what "exactly" means, and module 8's harness is what turns "approximately" into a number.

meaning is a convention; arrangement is a cost.

Here the cost is paid in a currency the earlier modules never used: not time, not memory, but correctness, in measured amounts, on purpose.

Read further, then check yourself

Grainger, Turnbull & Irwin, AI-Powered Search. The dense-retrieval and hybrid chapters, for where an approximate index sits in a real system rather than on its own.

Lin, Nogueira & Yates, Pretrained Transformers for Text Ranking. For how a reranker sits on top of an approximate first stage, which is module 15's subject and the standard answer to "my recall is only 86%".

  1. Recall fell to 86% but NDCG fell by only 0.0704. Explain the gap, and say which number you would put in a service-level objective.
  2. A filter matching 1.0% of documents broke the graph. At what selectivity would you expect the graph to become worth using again, and how would you find that point for your own data?
  3. Your team wants "the fastest possible vector search". What do you need to know before that request means anything?
If what you saw didn't match

If your recall is 1.0000 at every setting, your corpus is probably too small for the graph to have any choices to get wrong. Under a few tens of thousands of vectors, HNSW usually finds everything. Run at --scale part2 or larger before drawing conclusions from the curve.

If HNSW is slower than brute force, that is a real result at small scale and not a mistake. Brute force is one large matrix multiply, which modern hardware is extremely good at. The graph walk is many small dependent lookups, which it is not. The crossover is somewhere in the hundreds of thousands of vectors on most machines. Find yours.

If hnswlib will not install on macOS, it compiles C++ and needs the libc++ headers on the compiler's search path. requirements.txt has the one-line export that fixes it.

If your filtered recall looks fine at low ef_search, check that your filter is actually selective. A filter matching half the corpus costs the graph almost nothing. The cliff is a property of selectivity, not of filtering.
