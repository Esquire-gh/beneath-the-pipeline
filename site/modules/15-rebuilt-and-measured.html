15 · The pipeline, rebuilt and measured

All thirty lines of Part 0, now from parts you made. And then the reckoning: the same pipeline run on documents that fight back.

What this module is about

This is the module where the parts come back together. You assemble a complete retrieval pipeline from what you built: module 8's BM25, module 6's embeddings, module 7's index, and module 8's evaluation harness. Then you add the two upgrades that every production system reaches for. The first combines your two retrievers, because BM25 and dense retrieval win different queries and the standard advice is to fuse their rankings. The second is a reranker, a slower and more accurate model that reorders a shortlist the cheap stages produced.

Then comes the honest part, and it is the reason this module exists. Every evaluation so far, every number in Part II and Part III, ran on clean text that nobody had to fight for. Real corpora contain scans, hard layouts, and documents that extract to nothing, silently. So the final experiment runs your entire pipeline on a corpus built to fight back, three times, changing only which program turns the PDFs into text, and compares how much that choice matters against how much the retrieval architecture matters. The answer decides where your effort should actually go.

The puzzle

BM25 wins some queries. Dense retrieval wins others. Every guide says to fuse them, so you fuse them. Does the combined ranking actually beat the better of the two?

And the larger question underneath it: after fourteen modules of tuning what happens after the text exists, how much do any of those decisions matter, compared with the decision of how the text got extracted in the first place?

Build it yourself

Assemble the pipeline from what you built, then add the two new pieces.

Reciprocal rank fusion combines two ranked lists without needing their scores to be comparable, and BM25 scores and cosine similarities are not comparable; they live on different scales entirely. Ranks are comparable. A document's fused score is the sum of 1/(60 + its rank) across the lists.

Then a cross-encoder: a model that reads the query and one document together and scores the pair. That is why it is accurate, and it is also why it cannot be an index. There is nothing to precompute, so it can only be run over a shortlist that something cheaper produced.

Read the third row again. Fusion made things worse. Dense alone scored 0.8548, and fusing BM25 into it gave 0.7896, a loss of -0.0652.

That is not a bug in the fusion, and it is not a mistake in the measurement. Fusion combines two rankings as equals. On this benchmark dense retrieval is far stronger than BM25, 0.8548 against 0.6649, so averaging their ranks drags the strong one down toward the weak one. Fusion pays when the two are comparable in strength and disagree about which queries they win. Here they are not comparable, and it costs.

Module 8's lesson arrives one last time: the change that everyone recommends is still a hypothesis until you measure it on your corpus. Had this page reported the staged gains everyone expects, it would have been reporting a story rather than a measurement.

The reranker then earns its place, taking the fused list to 0.8920, the best number on the page and above dense alone. It reads each query and document together instead of comparing precomputed points, which is exactly what an index cannot do.

And it costs 4× the fused query it reorders. That is the shape of every production ranking stack: something cheap and approximate produces a shortlist, and something expensive and accurate reorders it. Module 12's approximate index is the cheap stage, and this is the expensive one.

Then the documents fight back

Everything above ran on clean text. Now take twenty documents whose true text is known, because the repository wrote it, draw them in four different layouts, and print half of them to images the way a scanner would. Then run the entire pipeline, extract, chunk, embed, index, retrieve, rerank, evaluate, three times, changing only one thing: which program turns the PDFs into text.

Every number in the first two rows is dragged down by the same fact: those extractors returned nothing for 10 of the 20 documents, so half the corpus was invisible to the pipeline. Not badly indexed. Absent, silently, with no error anywhere. The third row detects that failure and falls back to OCR, and recovers those documents at 100%.

The spread between extractors is 3.8× the spread between retrieval architectures.

And here is the sharpest form of it, because it is the one that should change what you do next:

The worst retrieval architecture on the good extraction, dense with no fusion and no reranker, scores 0.8495.

The best retrieval architecture on the broken extraction, reranked with everything switched on, scores 0.4938.

That is a gap of 0.3557 that no retrieval architecture closes, because the documents were not there to retrieve.

be precise about what this shows 50% of this corpus is scanned, which is a choice made to put the effect where it can be seen. On a corpus of clean digital PDFs the extractor choice would matter far less, and on a corpus of archived paper it would matter more. The magnitude depends on your documents; the direction does not. The measurement you owe your own corpus is the one on the left of that table — how many documents yield no text — and almost nobody takes it.

Where this leaves you

Two findings, both earned with your own numbers.

The pipeline's quality is decided upstream, at parse()

The line every tutorial spends the least time on. The one with no canonical book behind it. The one whose difficulty module 13 gave you four mechanisms for, and which this module shows outweighs every retrieval decision downstream of it.

It is also the only stage that fails silently. A bad chunk size shows up in your evaluation. A wrong ef_search shows up as recall. A document that extracted to an empty string shows up as nothing at all. The corpus is quietly smaller than you think, and every metric you compute is computed over the documents that survived.

And the pipeline's shape is a choice

Module 14 showed a whole class of question that similarity search cannot answer at any level of quality, because nearness was the only promise the convention ever made. Facts, counts, totals and relationships want data structured at ingestion: records, tables, and for questions that hop between things, graphs.

Production systems are routers over differently shaped stores. "Retrieval-augmented" never had to mean "similarity search". It meant fetch the right context, and similarity is one way to decide what right means.

How this fits the pipeline

Upward: there is nothing above this. You have rebuilt Part 0's 30 lines from parts you wrote, measured what each one costs, and found the one that matters most.

Backward: all of it. Module 1's blocks under module 4's syscalls under module 9's spill-and-merge. Module 2's agreements under module 5's parsing under module 13's ladder. Module 3's floats under module 6's embeddings under module 12's approximation. Module 7's arrangement under module 8's measurement under everything that claimed an improvement afterwards.

meaning is a convention; arrangement is a cost.

Every module was one more place where that was true, and you have the numbers for all of them.

Read further, then check yourself

Lin, Nogueira & Yates, Pretrained Transformers for Text Ranking. The cross-encoder you just used, properly: why reading query and document together beats comparing precomputed points, and why that means it can only ever be a second stage.

Huyen, AI Engineering. The operational envelope around all of it: evaluation you can run continuously, drift, cost, and the parts of a system that are neither model nor index.

Next step beyond this site: Designing Data-Intensive Applications, 2nd edition, on replication and partitioning. Everything here ran on one machine, deliberately, because that constraint is what kept the mechanisms visible. Growth past one machine is the named next subject.

  1. Fusion lowered NDCG on this benchmark. Describe the corpus and query mix where you would expect it to help, and the measurement that would tell you which case you are in.
  2. Your retrieval quality is disappointing on a new corpus. Name the first three measurements you would take, in order, and say what each would rule out.
  3. A colleague proposes switching embedding models to fix quality. Given the table above, what would you ask them before agreeing?
If what you saw didn't match

If fusion helps on your run, that is a legitimate result and the more common one in the literature. It means your BM25 and dense retrievers are closer in strength than these are. Check their individual scores before concluding anything about fusion in general; the mechanism in the explanation predicts both outcomes.

If all three extractors score the same, check the empty-documents column. If it reads 0 for all of them, your corpus has no scanned documents in it and the module's second table has nothing to measure. Run python data/make_eval_docs.py to build the mixed set.

If the reranker is very slow, remember it is reading 50 query-document pairs per query through a transformer. Lower --rerank-pool, or accept it: the cost is the point, and it is why reranking is always a second stage over a shortlist.

If your absolute NDCG values differ from these, compare shapes rather than numbers. The ordering of the extractor rows, and the fact that the gap between them exceeds the gap between architectures, is what the module claims.

A reader who holds both findings is no longer following tutorials. They are designing systems.
