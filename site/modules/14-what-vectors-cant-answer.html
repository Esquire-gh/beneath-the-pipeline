Part III · module 14 · build · 60–90 minutes

14 · What vectors can't answer

This module breaks retrieve() itself. Not its speed, its fitness. This is the last assumption in Part 0's inventory, and the only one that no amount of engineering fixes.

What this module is about

Every module so far has taken a question of the form "which documents are about X" and made answering it faster, cheaper, or more accurate. This module is about the questions that are not of that form at all. "How many invoices exceed $10,000?" "What is the total across all invoices from Acme?" "Which customers ordered from vendors who also supplied Acme?" These need counting, adding, filtering, and following relationships, and their answers are usually numbers that nobody ever wrote in any document.

Similarity search cannot answer them, and the reason is structural rather than a matter of quality. A retriever returns the few documents nearest a query. An aggregate question needs an operation applied across all of them, and there is no passage that is "most similar to" a sum. The fix is also structural: at ingestion, extract each document's facts into records with named, typed fields, put the records in a database, and answer those questions with the operations databases actually have. A system that handles both kinds of question is a router over differently shaped stores, and by the end of this module you will have built one and measured each part.

The puzzle

Take 300 invoices, parsed, chunked, embedded, and indexed. Ask your best pipeline, BM25 and dense retrieval fused:

"What is the total across all invoices from Acme Corp?"

It confidently returns the 4 most similar-sounding invoices. The true total is $677,283.85, and it is not written in any of them, or anywhere else in the corpus. Yet every number needed to compute it is in the corpus, parsed and indexed.

What is missing, and why won't better retrieval supply it?

Build it yourself

The corpus is 300 invoices rendered to PDF, and it exists so that every fact about it is known: the generator wrote the numbers before it drew the pages. The PDFs are the only input to the pipeline, and the ground truth is only ever used for scoring.

There are two question sets, both with exact answers:

  • 60 descriptive questions, like "which invoice from Contoso to Cascade Foundry includes a bearing assembly?" The answer is a document.
  • 24 aggregate questions, like "how many invoices exceed $10,000?" and "what is the total across all invoices from Acme Corp?" The answer is a number nobody wrote down.

Then build the fix: extract a structured record from each parsed invoice, load the records into SQLite, and answer the aggregate set with SQL. Then build a router that decides, per question, which store to ask.

The schema is yours. Which fields does a record hold? Are line items their own table? This is module 2's lesson with you on the authoring side one more time, and it has the same consequence: every field you leave out is a question the table can never answer.

What you should see

First, that the extraction is not the problem

An argument about what retrieval cannot do is worthless if the data was mangled on the way in. So grade the extractor against what the generator wrote:

That is deliberate: it removes parsing as an explanation, and everything that follows is about the shape of the question rather than the quality of the data. Similarity search is near perfect on descriptive questions and scores zero on aggregates. SQL is exactly inverted. And the zero deserves an explanation rather than an assertion, because it is the module's whole claim.

Why the zero is a measurement, not an assumption

Two things were measured, and neither of them needed a language model.

Evidence coverage: 9.9%. To answer "how many invoices exceed $10,000" you have to see every invoice that does. The median aggregate question here needs 36 documents, and the retriever returns 4. Even a perfect retriever handing back the 4 most relevant invoices delivers a tenth of the evidence.

The answer is not in the text: 0 of 10. For every aggregate question whose answer is a distinctive number, a total like $677,283.85 rather than a two-digit count that could match by coincidence, the retrieved text does not contain it anywhere. Not once. Because nobody ever wrote it down.

And the router beats both stores alone

The router is a dozen words of regular expression, "how many", "total", "average", "largest", "exceed", and it picks the right store for 100.0% of the 84 questions. That is a suspiciously easy result and you should treat it as one: these questions were generated from templates, so their phrasing is regular in a way real user questions are not. What survives the caveat is the shape. The routed system beats both stores alone, and it does so by a margin neither store could close by getting better.

Why it behaves this way

Go back to module 6, because the answer was already there.

An embedding is a learned convention for turning text into a point, arranged so that nearness of points tracks nearness of meaning. That is the entire promise. It says nothing about counting, nothing about summing, nothing about exactness, and nothing about relationships between documents, because nearness was the only promise the convention ever made.

So ask what an aggregate question actually needs. "How many invoices exceed $10,000" needs a comparison applied to every record and the results counted. "What is the total across all invoices from Acme" needs a filter, then an addition over 101 numbers. Neither is a question about which document is about something. There is no passage that is "most similar to" a sum.

And that is why retrieval fails here in a way more retrieval cannot fix. Raise k from 4 to 50 and evidence coverage improves, but the arithmetic still has not happened. Use a better embedding model and the points move slightly. Add a reranker and you reorder documents that were never the answer. The failure is not in the quality of the retrieval. It is that the question wanted an operation the store does not have.

What the table has that the index does not

The table has records: named fields with types, one row per thing, so a comparison can be applied to a column and the results counted. That is not a smarter index. It is a different shape of data, decided at ingestion, and it is what makes counting and summing expressible at all.

There is a third shape worth naming, because the questions that need it are common. When a question hops between things, "which customers ordered from vendors who also supplied Acme?", what you want is an entity-relationship graph: nodes for the things, edges for the relationships between them, so a query can follow a chain rather than filtering a column. It is the same move with a different geometry: structure built at ingestion so a class of question becomes cheap.

Which is the module's real subject. Production retrieval systems are routers over differently shaped stores: a vector index for "what is this about", an inverted index for exact terms, a relational table for facts and totals, sometimes a graph for relationships. Not one vector index with better prompts in front of it.

"Retrieval-augmented" never had to mean "similarity search". It meant fetching the right context. Similarity is one way to decide what the right context is, and it is the right way for exactly one kind of question.

The design question this leaves you with

You cannot route to a store you did not build, and you cannot build a table for fields you did not extract. So the decision moves upstream, to ingestion, and it is not a technical one: which questions is this system for?

If nobody will ever ask for a total, the table is wasted work. If somebody will, then no amount of retrieval quality will substitute for it, and the time to find that out is before the corpus is indexed rather than after a user asks.

How this fits the pipeline

Upward: the last line of Part 0 is demystified and demoted. retrieve() is one strategy, not the definition. Part 0's inventory ended with "is the nearest chunk even the right kind of answer to every question?", and the answer, measured, is no, for 24 of the 84 questions on this page.

Backward: module 1's filesystem keeps bookkeeping so a name becomes cheap to resolve. Module 7's index keeps bookkeeping so a word becomes cheap to find. This module's table keeps bookkeeping so a total becomes cheap to compute. It is the same move at three scales, and you have now built all three.

meaning is a convention; arrangement is a cost.

Here the cost of not arranging is the one that shows: the numbers were all present, and no amount of searching could add them up.

Read further, then check yourself

Kleppmann & Riccomini, Designing Data-Intensive Applications, 2nd edition, the opening chapters on data models and query languages. The formal home of "the shape of your data determines the questions you can ask". Expect the relational, document and graph models to be presented as answers to different question shapes, which is exactly the choice this module made you make.

Grainger, Turnbull & Irwin, AI-Powered Search. On combining structured signals with search, which is the production version of the router you just wrote.

  1. Your pipeline answers "what did Acme order in March?" well and "how much did Acme spend in March?" not at all. Explain the difference to a colleague without using the word "embedding".
  2. Raising k from 4 to 100 would raise evidence coverage. Would it make the aggregate answers correct? Say why.
  3. You are designing ingestion for a corpus of contracts. Name three fields you would extract into a table, and one question each would make cheap that retrieval alone would make impossible.
If what you saw didn't match

If your extraction accuracy is below 100%, your regular expressions are meeting a layout variation. Check which field failed and on which invoice. This corpus is deliberately uniform, so a failure here is a bug in the extractor rather than a hard document, and finding it is a fair use of an hour.

If SQL scores below 100% on aggregates, compare your query against the sql_hint field in ground_truth.json, which names the operation each question wants. A mismatch is usually rounding: totals are stored to the cent, so compare with a tolerance rather than with ==.

If retrieval does better than 0 on aggregates in your own version, check how you are grading it. Counting a two-digit answer as "present in the retrieved text" will find it by coincidence in almost any invoice. The solution only counts distinctive answers for exactly this reason.

If your router misclassifies, look at which side it errs on. Sending a descriptive question to SQL loses you a good answer. Sending an aggregate to the retriever produces a confident wrong one. Those are not equally bad, and a real router should be biased accordingly.
