Part II · module 6 · build · 40–60 minutes

06 · Chunk & Embed — how text becomes vectors

This module opens up two lines of the Part 0 pipeline: split(), which cuts documents into pieces, and embed(), which turns each piece into numbers that a computer can compare.

What these stages do, and why the pipeline needs them

By this point the pipeline has text. Module 5 pulled it out of the PDFs, and what came out is a handful of long strings, one per document. The next two stages prepare that text for retrieval, and each one exists to solve a practical problem.

The first problem is size. When someone asks a question, you want to hand the language model the paragraph that answers it, not the whole manual. A full document is too big to be a useful unit of retrieval, so the pipeline cuts each one into smaller pieces called chunks. The goal is for each chunk to hold one retrievable idea. There are several ways to cut. The crudest way counts characters and cuts every 500, no matter what the cut lands on. More careful ways cut at sentence or paragraph boundaries, so that a cut never falls in the middle of a thought. The Part 0 pipeline uses the crude way, and in this module you will measure what that choice costs.

The second problem is comparison. To retrieve the right chunk, the pipeline needs to know which chunk is most similar in meaning to the question, and a computer cannot compare meanings directly. It can compare numbers. So the pipeline runs every chunk through an embedding model, a neural network that turns a piece of text into a fixed-length list of numbers, 384 of them here. The model was trained so that texts about similar things come out with similar numbers. Once every chunk is a list of numbers, "find the most relevant chunk" becomes arithmetic: compare the question's numbers against every chunk's numbers and take the closest.

That is the plan on paper. The puzzle is how those numbers actually behave.

The puzzle

Take two sentences: the cat sat on the mat and a feline rested upon soft carpet. Read them side by side and you will find that they share no words at all. A system that matches words would call them completely unrelated.

The pipeline scores their similarity at 0.550, far above the score it gives a genuinely unrelated sentence.

If the computer is not matching words, what is it comparing?

Build it yourself

Open exercises/06-chunk-embed/starter.py. It has four TODOs. The first two are about cutting text up, and the last two are about turning text into numbers.

Write two chunkers and compare what they break

The first chunker cuts every 500 characters with 100 characters of overlap, which is exactly what the Part 0 pipeline does. The second splits the text into sentences first, then packs whole sentences together up to the same size limit, so that no cut ever lands inside a sentence.

Then write a third function that inspects the results. It finds every chunk boundary that landed in the middle of a word and prints the word that got broken. This is how you will see the damage directly instead of taking it on faith.

Turn a sentence into numbers, and measure similarity by hand

Tokenize a sentence and look at the integers that come out. Run the embedding model and look at the floats that come out. Then compute cosine similarity yourself, with a dot product and two vector lengths, about five lines of code. The reason to do this by hand is that "similarity" should not remain a function you have never opened.

What you should see

The fixed-size chunker cuts words in half

67% of the fixed-size chunk boundaries landed inside a word: 106 broken words across 159 chunks. The sentence-boundary chunker broke 0.

A broken word is the mild version of the problem. Run the same chunker over the table document from module 5 and the boundary lands here:

Neither half of that table can answer a question about the table. Both halves will still be embedded, indexed, and returned to users as confident matches.

Each word becomes an integer from a learned vocabulary

This is the same move you saw in module 2, where an agreement says that 65 means A. The difference is who made the agreement. The ASCII table was written by a committee. This vocabulary was learned from data during training, which is how 4937 came to mean cat. And when the vocabulary meets a word it has never seen, it takes the word apart into smaller pieces that it does know:

Each sentence becomes 384 floating-point numbers

The model turns those integers into 384 floats per sentence, which takes 1.5KB in float32. Every one of those floats is the kind of approximate number that module 3 examined. Here are the first eight, for the cat sentence:

The chart holds two results, and the second one matters more than the first.

  • The promise is kept. The cat sentence and the feline sentence share [], meaning no words at all, and they still score 0.550. The unrelated business sentence scores 0.013. Word matching would have called the first pair completely dissimilar, and the model saw through that.
  • The promise has a boundary. the cat did not sit on the mat is a flat denial of the first sentence, and it scores 0.868. That is higher than the true paraphrase. The model was never asked to encode truth. It encodes what a sentence is about, and a denial is about exactly the same thing as the claim it denies.

Why it behaves this way

An embedding is a learned convention for turning text into a point in space, chosen so that nearness of points tracks nearness of meaning. The model itself is the agreement. Nothing else in the pipeline knows what the numbers mean. They mean whatever the training made them mean.

Follow the path your sentence took. It became 8 integers through a lookup table, which is an agreement learned from a corpus rather than published by a standards body, and the ## pieces are how that table copes with words it never saw. Those integers went through the network and came out as 384 floats. Each float is an IEEE 754 approximation, which was module 3's subject, so the similarity between two sentences is really a sum of 384 approximate products. That is why two mathematically identical ways of computing the same similarity can disagree in the last decimal place.

Cosine similarity measures the angle between two of these points and ignores their length. That choice is deliberate. It means a long document and a short one about the same subject can score alike, which is usually what you want, and occasionally the reason a one-line chunk beats a full paragraph.

What an embedding promises, exactly

The negation result is the one to hold onto, because it defines the boundary of the whole technique. The model puts the cat sat on the mat and the cat did not sit on the mat almost on top of each other, at 0.868, because both sentences are about the same cat, the same mat, and the same sitting. Truth is not part of the arrangement. Neither is quantity, neither is exactness, and neither is any relation between one document and another.

So state the promise precisely. An embedding promises that text about similar things lands in similar places. It promises nothing else. It does not promise that near things agree with each other, that near things are true, or that anything can be counted. Module 14 is what happens when a question needs one of those, and this is the sentence it cashes out.

Chunking decides what gets a vector at all

Now put the two halves of this module together, because they are not separate topics.

Embedding turns a chunk into one point, so everything inside the chunk gets averaged into a single position. That means the chunk boundary decides what counts as "a thing" in your system. The 67% of boundaries that fell inside a word are the small version of the problem. The large version is the table above: half a row, with its header in a different chunk, embedded as a point that is about nothing in particular, and still returned to users as a confident match.

Fixed-size chunking is not a neutral default. It is a decision to cut wherever the character count runs out, and its one real virtue is that it needs no knowledge of the document. Every alternative, whether sentences, paragraphs, headings, or table rows, is a bet that you can detect a structure in the text. Module 5 has already shown you how reliably you can detect structure in a PDF.

How this fits the pipeline

Upward: split() and embed() are two decisions dressed up as two function calls. One picks what a unit of meaning is, and the other picks what meaning is. Backward: module 2 said text is a convention over bytes, and module 3 said numbers are. This module is both at once. Text goes in under a learned table, and numbers come out under a published float format.

meaning is a convention; arrangement is a cost.

Ahead: module 7 takes these 384-dimensional points and asks what it costs to find the nearest one. Module 12 finds out what you give up when there are a million of them.

Read further, then check yourself

Grainger, Turnbull & Irwin, AI-Powered Search, the dense-retrieval chapters. Expect them to treat the embedding model as one component among several rather than as the whole system, which is the framing module 14 will need.

  1. Two sentences share no words and score 0.550. Explain to a colleague what is being compared, without using the word "semantic".
  2. A denial scores higher than a paraphrase. Is the model broken? Say what it was actually asked to do.
  3. Your chunks are 500 characters. Someone proposes 2000 to "keep more context". Name one thing that improves and one that gets worse, in terms of what a single vector can represent.
If what you saw didn't match

If your cosine values differ from these in the third decimal place, that is expected. Versions of sentence-transformers and different hardware backends round differently, and module 3 explains why. The ordering should hold: paraphrase well above unrelated, negation high.

If the negation does not score above the paraphrase for you, try a different model. Some are trained on natural-language-inference data and handle negation better. The finding is about what a given model promises, not about all models, so check yours rather than assuming.

If your hand-written cosine disagrees with numpy's, the usual cause is integer division or summing in a different order. verify.py compares against numpy to a tolerance of 1e-6 and prints both values.
