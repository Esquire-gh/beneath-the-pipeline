Part 0 · the frame

Beneath the Pipeline

A retrieval pipeline is 30 lines of Python sitting on decades of storage engineering, byte-level convention, and information-retrieval research. You are about to build every one of those floors yourself, at small scale, and then push each one until it breaks.

0a · The pipeline, working

Here is the pipeline the internet teaches. Open PDFs, pull out their text, cut the text into chunks, turn each chunk into a list of numbers, put those numbers in a database, and ask the database for the four chunks nearest your question.

Run it against the folder of PDFs in this repository:

It works. On this machine it read 5 PDFs, which is 44 pages and 3.5MB on disk. It pulled out 129,591 characters of text, cut them into 165 chunks, turned each chunk into 384 numbers, indexed them, and answered a question in 731.7ms. The top passage it returned came from arxiv-transformer.pdf, and it is the right passage:

Say it plainly: for a demo, this is fine. Nothing here is wrong. Thousands of working systems start exactly like this, and if your corpus is small and your questions are the kind this shape of pipeline suits, you can stop reading and ship it.

The question this site asks is not what is wrong with these lines. It is: how many things did you just take on faith?

0b · The inventory of assumptions

Walk the pipeline line by line. Each line does something you could not explain to someone who asked. Here is the literal list.

This list is the table of contents. Every question below names the module that answers it, and the modules are in pipeline order.

  • load(pdf_dir)

    What is a file? Your program asked for one and got bytes back. Where were those bytes, what told the machine where to find them, and what did the asking cost?

    And why do the bytes on disk become text at all? Who decided that this byte means that letter?

    01 · what is a file 02 · how text becomes bytes 04 · load
  • parse(docs)

    Why is extracting text from a PDF a step? The text is right there on the screen. Why does a program have to work for it?

    And why do two libraries reading the same file disagree about what it says?

    05 · parse 13 · why parsing is hard
  • split(parsed, size=1000)

    Why cut the text up at all? Who chose 1000, and what happens to a table that the cut lands in the middle of?

    06 · chunk & embed
  • embed(chunks, model)

    How did text become 384 numbers? What is a number here, how is it stored, and how exact is it?

    And what does distance between two of these lists of numbers actually promise you?

    03 · how numbers become bytes 06 · chunk & embed 12 · approximation
  • db.add(...)

    What did the database build? Something was constructed, it took time, and it takes up room. What is it, and why does having it make asking for neighbours fast?

    What happens when it no longer fits in memory, or when new documents arrive after it was built?

    07 · index 09 · it doesn't fit 10 · ingestion vs query
  • retrieve(db, model, q, k=4)

    Top four, ranked by what? Say a query matches four thousand passages. Four come back. Which four, and who decided?

    How would you know if the ranking is bad? What does it cost to rank a million passages instead of a hundred thousand?

    And the last assumption, the one nobody states: is "the nearest chunk" even the right kind of answer to every question?

    08 · retrieve 11 · at scale 14 · what vectors can't answer

0c · The map

The pipeline, drawn once. Each stage carries the module where you build it and the modules where you break it. This drawing is also the strip along the top of every page: as you work, stages fill in, and Part III marks the ones you have stressed.

The modules

  • Part I · the floor — nothing to install, 15–25 minutes each
  • 01What is a file, actually?What the operating system really keeps when you save a file, and why three characters occupy 4096 bytes.
  • 02How text becomes bytesThe agreements that turn bytes into letters, and what happens when a reader applies the wrong one.
  • 03How numbers become bytesHow numbers, and through them audio and images, are written into bytes, and why 0.1 + 0.2 is not 0.3.
  • Part II · the pipeline, one line at a time
  • 04Load — what reading a file costsWhy reading ten thousand small files costs far more than reading the same bytes from one big file.
  • 05Parse — how a PDF becomes textWhat is actually inside a PDF, and why two libraries reading the same file return different text.
  • 06Chunk & Embed — how text becomes vectorsHow text gets cut into chunks and turned into numbers, so that similar meanings land near each other.
  • 07Index — how search gets fastWhat db.add() built: the precomputed structure that turns seconds of scanning into microseconds of lookup.
  • 08Retrieve — how results get rankedThe scoring formulas that decide which four passages come back, and how to measure whether they are any good.
  • Part III · when reality arrives
  • 09When the index doesn't fit in memoryHow to build an index bigger than your RAM, by spilling to disk and merging.
  • 10Keeping the index fresh as documents arriveWhy adding documents to a finished index is hard, and how segments and merging solve it.
  • 11How ranking stays fast at scaleHow search engines skip the documents that cannot reach the top ten, without changing the answer.
  • 12How vector search scalesThe approximate index that makes a million vectors searchable, and exactly what it gives up in return.
  • 13Why parsing is a hard problemTwo PDFs that look identical on screen, and why one parses perfectly while the other returns nothing.
  • 14What vectors can't answerQuestions that need counting, joining, or exactness, and the structured tools that answer them.
  • 15The pipeline, rebuilt and measuredYour own parts put back together, then measured against documents that fight back.

The machine these numbers came from

Every number printed on this site was produced by running the code on one machine. Here it is. Your absolute numbers will differ, and that is expected. What the site actually claims are the ratios, one strategy against another, one index against another, and those hold across machines. Where a ratio depends on your hardware, the page says so.

Every exercise takes a --scale flag. The default is small enough to finish on a laptop without filling its memory; the pages name the scale their own numbers came from. If your machine is smaller than this one, run the default and read the ratios. If it is larger, push the scale up and watch the same shapes appear.

The contract

By the end of this site you will have rebuilt these 30 lines yourself, from parts you wrote. You will know what every one of them costs. And you will know the questions they cannot answer.

The order never varies. Part I lays the floor: what a disk holds, what a file is, and how numbers and text become bytes by agreement. Part II walks the pipeline one stage at a time and has you build an honest small version of each. Part III turns the assumptions off, first scale and then the two hard truths the demo hides, and the last module puts your own parts back together and measures them against documents that fight back.

meaning is a convention; arrangement is a cost.

That sentence is the whole site. Every module is one more place where it is true. Start at the floor.
